> [!NOTE] This essay is an edited transcription of a sermon on Genesis 3.

Advent is a season of waiting. We enter the story of the people of God as they anticipate the birth of Christ, sitting with all the Old Testament hopes that lead towards Jesus.

But we also wait from the other side of his coming. Jesus has been born, died and risen. He is seated at the right hand of the Father, interceding for us as our saviour and champion. The kingdom of God is coming in all sorts of ways, and yet the world around us is still not as it is supposed to be. We wait for the day when Jesus will come and make all things new.

To understand why the birth of Jesus is such a dawn of hope, we need the backstory. We need to see not only that Jesus saves us from sin, but that he comes to restore the whole of creation—and to restore us to the people God made us to be.

## The God of the cosmos and the God of the compost

Genesis gives us two creation accounts. In the first, God creates by speaking. It is declarative: God speaks, and things come into being. When God creates humanity, God says, “Let us make humankind in our image.”

In the second account, God reaches into the dirt and forms the human from the earth. We meet both the God of the cosmos and the God of the compost: the God of all power and might, who can speak creation into being, and the God who comes close like a craftsperson shaping clay. God breathes into the human’s nostrils and gives him life. It is startlingly intimate.

Together, these accounts say something extraordinary about the dignity of being human. We are made in the image of God. We are placed within creation like angled mirrors: reflecting the praise of creation towards God and reflecting the glory and love of God towards creation.

That privilege gives us a purpose. We are here to show creation, and one another, who God is. We worship on behalf of creation and serve creation on behalf of God.

This is a communal calling. Sometimes collectively praising God means carrying people who cannot presently sing. Sometimes collectively serving creation means carrying people who do not have the capacity to contribute in the same way as others. We need one another precisely because we are not all able to do the same things.

The human beings in Genesis 2 are placed in a garden and given three beautiful connections:

- They are connected to God, walking with God in the garden.
- They are connected to creation. The Hebrew word often translated “till” in the instruction to work the ground can also mean “serve.” Humanity is placed in service to creation, entrusted with God’s loving care for it.
- They are connected to one another. The man greets the woman with poetry: “bone of my bones and flesh of my flesh.”

Connection to God, connection to creation and connection to one another: this is the picture of humanity before the fall. This is who we are.

## The fall is not our deepest identity

The lies of evil are not who we are.

Our deepest identity is the dignity God has given us: to bear God’s image, reflect God’s glory to creation and offer creation’s praise back to God. Everything that follows in Genesis 3—the serpent, the lies, the shame and fear—distorts that identity, but it does not erase what God has said.

The fall does not change God’s declaration about humanity. Even when we fail to live as God’s image-bearers, we remain people God has created and called.

Genesis 3 shows the man and woman becoming ashamed, afraid and alone. The gospel meets these distortions with pride, peace and presence.

“Pride” may sound provocative. There is a pride that grasps at superiority, puts us above other people or tries to take God’s place. That is not what I mean. I mean the opposite of shame: knowing the dignity God has given us. God wants us to know that we are God’s beloved creation, fearfully and wonderfully made.

## The snake’s first lie

The serpent begins with a question: “Did God really say that you must not eat from any tree in the garden?”

No. God did not say that. God gave them every tree in the garden except one.

The serpent’s first move is to question God’s goodness and provision. God is recast as a controlling tyrant, and freedom is imagined as something humanity can only find by divorcing itself from God and going its own way.

The woman corrects the serpent, but even her answer enlarges the prohibition: she says that they must not eat from the tree or even touch it. That was not what God said either. The generous abundance of God’s gift is disappearing from view while the single boundary grows larger.

The serpent’s lie is that God is not a good father. Jesus later asks which father would give his child a stone when asked for bread, or a snake when asked for a fish. How much more, he says, will our Father in heaven give good gifts?

Genesis 3 reveals the cost of believing the opposite.

The woman sees that the fruit is good, takes it and gives some to the man. That word “good” should remind us of the creation story. Until now, God has been the one who sees and declares what is good. Here, human beings turn away from God’s wisdom and seize the right to determine good and evil for themselves.

## From shame to pride

Their eyes are opened, they realise that they are naked, and they cover themselves.

Shame is not the same as guilt. Guilt concerns what we have done: *That was wrong. I need forgiveness, and I need to make it right.* Shame concerns who we are: *I am wrong. I am unlovable. I need to hide.*

The first can lead to repentance and repair. The second contradicts what God has spoken over us. Our failures do not override God’s word that we are made in God’s image.

I have ADHD. That is not the whole of my identity, but it describes something true about the way my brain works. It brings real challenges. I can be scatty. I can put something down and immediately have to run around looking for it. Those moments can generate a great deal of shame.

For a long time, I imagined these were aspects of me that God loved me in spite of. Therapy helped me begin to understand that the same mind also gives me gifts I am grateful for. God does not merely tolerate the inconvenient parts while loving an imaginary, tidier version of me. God loves me because God made me to be me.

God loves you because God made you to be you.

There will always be things in us that need healing and habits that need repentance. But shame tells a deeper lie: that God does not love every part of the person God created. The answer to that lie is a holy pride—a settled knowledge of the dignity God has given us.

## From fear to peace

When God comes into the garden, the man says, “I heard you, and I was afraid because I was naked, so I hid.”

Fear is not itself a sin. It is often a signal that our understanding of the world is no longer aligned with the reality God has promised. It can then drive us towards choices that harm ourselves and others.

Fear tells us that there will not be enough. It tells us to grasp, compete and protect ourselves. It imagines the world as a contest in which only the strongest survive. But that is not the way of the image-bearer, and it is not the way of Jesus.

The fear of the Lord is different from terror. It recognises that God is vast, majestic and powerful—and therefore that we can rest in God’s goodness.

I remember standing by a lighthouse on a fiercely windy day. Waves crashed against the shore and the sea felt enormous. The noise and scale of it were enough to make me uneasy even though I knew I was safe. My son Ezra, still a baby, slept peacefully against me. He did not need to understand the sea. He only needed to know that he was close to someone who held him.

Perhaps we need to become more like that in our relationship with God: awake to the magnitude of what surrounds us, but able to rest because we know who carries us.

## From isolation to presence

God asks the man whether he has eaten from the tree. The man answers: “The woman whom you gave to be with me—she gave me fruit from the tree, and I ate.”

In a single sentence he blames the woman and blames God.

When we feel threatened, our instinct is often to cover our own backs and separate ourselves from other people. Fear of being seen as weak, inadequate or guilty can make us push blame down onto the people we were supposed to protect.

The beautiful connections of Genesis 2 are unravelled. The man pushes away the woman and pushes away God. Humanity ends up alone.

This is not what God wants for us, and it is not who God says we are. We were made for connection with God, relationship with one another and loving service of creation. The consequences named in Genesis 3 show each of those relationships breaking down.

The relationship between human beings becomes hierarchical. Men and women were created equally in God’s image, but now domination enters the story. The same distortion appears in every human hierarchy that assigns greater worth to some people and less to others.

The relationship with the earth becomes painful. The ground produces thorns. Whenever fear convinces us that there is not enough, we hoard wealth and power at creation’s expense. Today the climate crisis makes this disconnection visible on a global scale. Creation groans as it waits for the children of God to live like the image-bearers we were made to be.

The relationship with God is fractured too. It is not simply that God cannot bear to be near sinners; sin cannot survive unchanged in the presence of God. Humanity is kept from the tree of life because living for ever in this state of disconnection would not be a gift. God intends something better than an endless life of shame, fear and isolation.

## The promise of the snake crusher

In the middle of these consequences comes a promise. God tells the serpent that there will be enmity between the serpent and the woman, and between their offspring: the serpent will strike his heel, but he will crush the serpent’s head.

The story names an experience common to humanity. There is a person I want to be, yet there is also the person I find myself becoming. We try to do what is right and discover resistance within ourselves and in the systems around us. We may act with integrity at work and be penalised for it. We may challenge abuse and find ourselves isolated. We wrestle with evil, and it strikes back.

Christian tradition sees in this promise a picture of Jesus: the one who conquers sin and evil and makes a way back into connection with God, one another and creation. On the cross Jesus crushes the power of evil, but he is wounded and killed in the act.

Jesus is the perfect image-bearer. He is everything humanity was called to be.

He lives with pride: not superiority, but an unshakable knowledge that he is the beloved Son of God.

He lives with peace. He tells people not to be anxious about tomorrow and invites them to trust the Father who feeds the birds and clothes the flowers.

He lives with presence. Although he carries the vocation of the world’s true king, he repeatedly withdraws to quiet places to pray. He refuses the lie that relentless activity is more important than connection with his Father.

Jesus lives in perfect pride, peace and presence, then shares that restored humanity with us.

## Grace is already present in the garden

Even in Genesis 3, God does not abandon humanity.

God preserves their vocation. The work of the ground will now be difficult, but humanity is still called to serve creation. We cannot refuse that calling simply because we know we cannot fulfil it perfectly. Forgiveness is free, and from that security we can step again into the work God has given us.

God gives them clothing. Fig leaves offer little protection from cold, weather or wild animals, so God provides what their anxious hiding cannot.

And God comes looking for them. God already knows what has happened, yet still walks in the garden and calls, “Where are you?” Sin brings disconnection, but God’s first movement is presence.

Finally, the way back to the tree of life is opened in Jesus. The cross becomes the tree from which we receive life. In communion we enter this truth again: when we come to Jesus and receive what he has done, we receive reconnection with the wisdom and life of God.

We do not need to be ashamed, afraid or alone. God is here. We can confess what we have done without believing that it defines who we are. We can trust the one who forgives us, restores us and directs us towards the life for which we were made.

Nothing—not trouble, hardship, persecution, hunger, nakedness, danger, death, life, the present or the future—can separate us from the love of God in Christ Jesus.
